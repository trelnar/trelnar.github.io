Oracle Health

Director, User Experience Design, 2024 to present.

I joined Oracle Health to lead the design of the core task-management experience for an AI-native EHR. It started with ambulatory clinical workflows and grew into one universal pattern for every role across the whole product. Out of that work I formed Experience Core, our platform design team, and built its AI workflows with engineering, which took concept-to-testable-prototype from weeks to days. That work produced eight AI patent applications I am a named inventor on.

Most of this is unreleased, so the cases are told through method and diagrams: no screens, no internal names, no customers.

From Inbox to Action List

It started as a fix for the clinician inbox, a twenty-year-old design where results, refills, documents, and requests each sat in their own folder. Looking closer, the inbox was one instance of a platform-wide habit: every product team had built its own worklist, each with different features, rules, and edge cases.

We flattened the inbox into a single “Action List” of the non-negotiable tasks a person owns that day, auto-triaged by role and by user. Once the list could absorb anything that needed doing, there was no reason for the other lists to exist.

Action List became the one universal worklist pattern across the whole EHR: every role, every workflow, from ambulatory, acute, patient administration, rev cycle and reimbursements. One place to see the work and do it, instead of a different queue in every corner of the product.

Experience Core

Oracle Health already had a patterns team and a shared goal of one design language. What it lacked was structure: thirty-four patterns, several managers, no single thread of ownership.

I rebuilt the team around the clinician’s journey instead of the pattern list. Six segments, each with a lead designer and research partner who own their segment, end to end. We called ourselves Experience Core.

The team runs on six habits. Concepts, components, and guidelines define what we make. Cadence, governance, and evangelism define how we work. Guidance lives where the work lives, not in redlines, but in a living, coded reference app.

AI-native UX: where the system speaks, and where it stops

One set of rules for how, and how much, the system talks to a clinician.

The system has something to say on nearly every screen, and a clinician has to tell a fact from a suggestion from a to-do. I drew the line: notifications assert facts, guidance proposes decisions, the worklist holds work.

I authored the notification pattern: one object model, a five-class severity taxonomy, an interruption ladder from ambient to a person. Alarm fatigue is a patient-safety risk, so the system interrupts only as much as the situation needs.

The system drafts, the clinician decides. A spoken list of problems, medications, and orders is teed up in one review space, signed once, and committed in the right order. Accept, edit, reject, escalate: four verbs on every AI surface.

Guided workflows are one pattern with two modes, structured and generative; the difference is who authors the sequence, and when. Several of the eight patent applications come from this thinking. The filings are confidential, so this page describes the reasoning, not the claims.
