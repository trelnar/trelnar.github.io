I joined Oracle Health to lead the design of the core task-management experience for an AI-native EHR. It started with ambulatory clinical workflows and grew into taking dozens of one-off legacy experiences and combining them into one universal pattern for every role across the whole product: ambulatory, acute, reimbursement, patient administration.

Out of that work I formed Experience Core, our platform design team. We set one design standard across the product organizations for shared components and interaction patterns, with tight integration into engineering so what we design is what ships. I also built the team's AI workflows with engineering, which took concept-to-testable-prototype from weeks to days. That work produced eight AI patent applications I am a named inventor on.

Three cases follow. Most of the work is unreleased and stays inside Oracle, so each is told through method, diagrams, and outcomes: no product screens, no internal names, no customers.

Case 1

One worklist for every role

The problem

Clinicians got their work from dozens of places: an inbox for messages, a results queue, orders waiting for a signature, requests from other roles, reminders from the system. Each had been built once, by one team, for one role, and each behaved differently. A physician learned one set of habits, a nurse another, the front desk a third, and the same item could show up in three of them.

The ask was to start with ambulatory clinical workflows. It grew into taking the one-off legacy experiences across the product and combining them into one universal pattern for every role.

Constraints

Safety first: anything the system interprets or suggests is teed up for a clinician to review, never committed on its own. Several product organizations, each with its own release train. A legal rule on certain words that shaped the naming. And legacy surfaces that had to keep working while the new pattern replaced them one at a time.

The approach

I designed the pattern first as a clinician inbox, and it was elevated to the platform worklist, the Action List. The rule underneath it is simple: work lands in one place, and the list knows what to do with each kind of work. A message is work until it is answered. A result is work until it is acknowledged. An order is work until it is signed. Messaging stopped being a separate application and became a kind of item in the list, answered in place and handed to the right role when the question belongs to someone else.

Roles configure the list; they do not fork it. The physician sees fewer items, weighted to decisions. The nurse sees more, weighted to execution and follow-up. The front desk sees scheduling and paperwork and nothing clinical. One pattern, three experiences, because the configuration knows what each role is there to do.

What changed

The Action List shipped in 2025 and is in customers' hands. When leadership relayed that customers were unhappy with it, with no specifics, I ran an investigation instead of reacting: product feedback gathered and reread, a diff of the design against the released build, usability testing, and a rule that no customer was contacted until the internal analysis was done. The finding was a taxonomy failure in our own feedback data. The same surface had four names across the company, and most of the feedback was filed under other components where nobody could find it. The team got a corrected reference set, and the fixes had evidence behind them.

My role

I designed the original pattern, wrote the skill that carries its rules, when it applies, what it holds, how it is configured, and led the investigation. The skill and a three-role demo became the reference example senior engineering leadership pointed to.

What I would do differently

Put the released build in front of the design team from the first sprint. The diff analysis found drift between what we designed and what shipped that a live reference build would have caught months earlier.

Case 2

Experience Core, and the AI workflow behind it

The problem

Every product organization designed its own version of the same things: lists, panels, notifications, search, page layouts. There was no platform design function, so there was no one standard, and engineering rebuilt the same component in several shapes. The worklist work made the gap obvious: a universal pattern needs a universal owner.

Constraints

The team did not exist. I had to make the case for it, staff it, and prove it, while a parallel AI effort ran its own track that leadership had not yet reconciled with ours. Clinician-facing software also carries accessibility and regulatory obligations that a pattern library has to satisfy once, for everyone.

Forming the team

I proposed Experience Core as the platform design team: shared components, interaction patterns, the design system, and the research practice, with one standard across the product organizations and tight integration into engineering so what we design is what ships. We inherited thirty-four patterns and no structure. I grouped them by the clinician's journey, access, triage, interpret, execute, collaborate, follow up, and assigned ownership by pattern, so every designer owned a stretch of journey rather than a pile of components. Alignment went in sequence: eight engineering and product pillar leads first, then SVP peers, then the top.

The AI workflow

With engineering I rebuilt how the team produces. The comp stopped being the deliverable; the rules did. Components get guidelines, reference a person or a model can look up. Layouts and patterns get skills, which carry the judgment of when, when not, and how. A skill selects and configures. It does not code. I wrote the first skills myself, then gave each designer a pattern and its skill. Around that sits a prototyping pipeline, custom tooling, and a shared skill library in the team repo. An intent goes in, a brief, a frame, a data file, and a working prototype comes out.

What changed

Concept to testable prototype went from weeks to days, and the team validates more concepts per cycle with customers, because a prototype is now cheap enough to be wrong. One design standard holds across the product organizations and dozens of clinician-facing applications, with WCAG 2.1 AA as a gate rather than a hope. A written definition of done, three gates and a release approval, ended the misalignment that had kept layouts from landing. The work produced eight AI patent applications I am a named inventor on.

My role

I proposed the team, built it, and lead it. I wrote the first skills and ran the learning day where every designer shipped a working prototype in one session, none of it product, all of it the new muscle.

What I would do differently

Write the definition of done before the first layout, not after the first misalignment. The gates were cheap to write and expensive to be without.

Case 3

AI-native UX: where the system speaks, and where it stops

The problem

In an AI-native EHR the system has something to say on nearly every screen: a result came back, a guideline applies, a draft is ready, a set of tasks could be bundled. Left to each product team, that becomes a different voice on every surface, and a clinician who cannot tell a fact from a suggestion from a to-do. I led the design for how and when the system surfaces AI output to clinicians across the clinical workflows.

Constraints

Nothing the system interprets or suggests commits without a clinician's review. That is a regulatory line as well as a design one. Alarm fatigue is a patient-safety risk, so every interruption has to earn its place. A legal constraint ruled out a word the whole industry uses, which forced better naming. And all of it has to be accessible.

Three patterns, one boundary

I drew the line between three things teams kept blurring. The notification pattern asserts facts: the state of the world changed. Guidance proposes decisions. The worklist holds work. A fact can lead to a proposal, and an accepted proposal becomes work, but each lives in its own pattern with its own rules. I authored the notification pattern for the whole product: one notification object model, a five-class severity taxonomy, and an interruption ladder with five rungs, ambient, passive, active, disruptive, and escalation to a person. The system interrupts only as much as the situation needs, and the rules say which situations need what.

Teed up, never auto-added

For AI output the rule is the same everywhere: the system drafts, the clinician decides. When an agent parses a spoken list of problems, medications, and orders, it tees them up in one review space where the clinician edits, removes, and signs once, and the system commits in the right order underneath, diagnoses before the orders that depend on them. Accept, edit, reject, escalate: those four verbs are the whole interaction, on every AI surface. Clinical leadership validated the direction.

Guided workflows got a point of view too: one pattern with two orchestration modes, structured and generative. The difference is who authors the sequence and when, not whether the screen adapts.

What changed

The patterns gave product teams anchors to build on instead of inventing their own, and gave engineering one set of behaviors to implement. The thinking here underlies several of the eight AI patent applications I am named on. The filings are confidential, so this page describes the reasoning, not the claims.

My role

Program owner for the notification pattern and its specification. Author of the guided-workflow point of view. Design lead for AI output across the clinical workflows, with the multi-add direction validated by clinical leadership.

What I would do differently

Name the modes earlier. "AI mode" lived as a label for too long before it was renamed for what it does, and every week it survived, teams designed to the label.
